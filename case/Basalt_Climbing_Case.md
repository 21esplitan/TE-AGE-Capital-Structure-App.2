# Basalt Climbing Co.: Capital Structure

*Basalt Climbing Co. is a fictional company.*

Basalt Climbing Co. runs an indoor climbing gym two blocks from campus. Two founders started it eight years ago and still own 40% of its 200,000 shares. About 60 local investors own the rest and trade the shares through a regional broker. The shares sell for $18.00 today.

The gym is as big as its building allows. In a normal year it sells the equivalent of 2,500 annual memberships at $1,000 each. With no room to expand, Basalt needs no new capital, pays out all of its earnings as dividends and expects its operating income (EBIT) to stay at its normal-year level for the foreseeable future.

Basalt has never borrowed. One of the founders has read that some companies raise their stock price by adding debt, and she wants to know whether that is true for Basalt and, if so, how much debt makes sense. She has asked you to find out.

## Operations

| | Normal year |
|---|---:|
| Memberships sold | 2,500 |
| Revenue | 2,500,000 |
| Variable costs (40% of revenue) | 1,000,000 |
| Fixed operating costs | 900,000 |
| **Operating income (EBIT)** | **600,000** |

Revenue changes from year to year. Variable costs stay at 40% of revenue and fixed costs at $900,000 in every kind of year. A strong year counts day passes and youth-team fees as membership equivalents.

| Kind of year | Probability | Memberships | Revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Weak | 0.25 | 1,750 | 1,750,000 |
| Normal | 0.50 | 2,500 | 2,500,000 |
| Strong | 0.25 | 3,250 | 3,250,000 |

|---|---:|

## Financing information

- Basalt's tax rate is 25% (federal and state combined).
- The risk-free rate is 5.0% and the market risk premium is 6.0%.
- A publicly traded climbing-gym chain has the same business risk as Basalt. Its levered beta is 1.625, its debt-to-equity ratio at market values is 0.40, and its tax rate is also 25%.
- A local investment banker estimates Basalt's costs of debt and equity at different amounts of borrowing:

| Amount borrowed | Cost of debt (r_d) | Cost of equity (r_s) |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | — | 12.5% |
| 600,000 | 6.0% | 13.5% |
| 1,200,000 | 6.5% | 15.0% |
| 1,800,000 | 7.5% | 18.0% |
| 2,400,000 | 9.0% | 24.0% |

- Any debt would be perpetual. The interest rate applies to the full amount borrowed.
- Basalt would use the money it borrows to buy back its own shares. Once Basalt announces the plan, the share price moves to its new level, and Basalt buys shares back at that new price. Shareholders who sell and shareholders who keep their shares get the same price.
- Ignore personal taxes, issue costs and other transaction costs.

## Questions

### A. Business risk and operating leverage

1. What revenue does Basalt need, and how many memberships, for EBIT to be zero?
2. What is Basalt's degree of operating leverage (DOL) in a normal year? Use it to predict EBIT in a weak year, then check your prediction against the table.
3. What is business risk? Name two things about running a climbing gym that create it.

### B. Financial leverage and financial risk

Compare Basalt as it is today with Basalt after it borrows $1,200,000 and buys back shares. Book equity falls by the amount spent on the shares.

4. For each kind of year, find EBIT, interest, net income, return on equity (ROE, using book equity) and times interest earned (TIE) under both structures.
5. Find the expected ROE, the standard deviation of ROE and the coefficient of variation under each structure, and the expected TIE with debt. What does debt do to the expected return and to the risk shareholders bear? What is financial risk, and how is it different from business risk?
6. Find the degree of financial leverage (DFL) and the degree of total leverage (DTL) in a normal year with $1,200,000 of debt. What do they tell the founders?

### C. Modigliani and Miller (MM)

7. Use the Hamada equation to unlever the comparable chain's beta. Then use the CAPM to find Basalt's cost of equity as an all-equity firm. Show that it is consistent with the $18.00 share price.
8. Suppose for a moment that there are no taxes at all. Using MM, find Basalt's value, its cost of equity and its weighted average cost of capital (WACC) if it borrows $1,200,000 at 6.5%. Explain homemade leverage: in this world, why wouldn't investors pay more for a levered Basalt?
9. Now bring back the 25% tax. Use MM with taxes to find Basalt's value and cost of equity at each amount of borrowing in the banker's table. Compare MM's cost of equity with the banker's. What could explain the difference?

### D. The optimal capital structure

10. For each amount of borrowing in the banker's table, find the interest, net income, value of the equity, value of the firm, new share price, shares repurchased, shares remaining, earnings per share (EPS) and TIE.
11. Which amount of borrowing gives the highest share price? Does the same amount give the highest EPS? Why or why not?
12. Find the WACC at each amount of borrowing, first with market-value weights and then with book-value weights. Which set of weights points to the structure with the highest share price? Why?
13. Compare the firm value from question 10 with MM's value from question 9 at each amount of borrowing. What does the difference measure, and how does it change as Basalt borrows more?
14. Find the EBIT at which EPS is the same with no debt and with $1,200,000 of debt (buying back shares at the price you found in question 10). Which structure gives the higher EPS in each kind of year?

### E. Dividend policy

15. If Basalt borrows $1,200,000 and keeps paying out all of its earnings, what is its dividend per share?
16. Instead of buying back shares, Basalt could borrow $1,200,000 and pay it all out as a special dividend on every share. What is each share worth after the dividend, and what is a shareholder's total wealth per share? Compare this with question 10. What does the comparison show about dividends and repurchases in MM's world, and what could make shareholders prefer one over the other?
17. The founders don't sell any shares in the buyback. What fraction of Basalt do they own after it buys back shares with $1,200,000? Why might the other investors care?
18. Why might investors read a debt-financed buyback as good news about Basalt?

### F. Recommendation

19. How much should Basalt borrow? Besides these numbers, what would you weigh before you recommend it, for example lenders' attitudes, keeping some borrowing capacity in reserve, the founders' control and the gym's business risk?
